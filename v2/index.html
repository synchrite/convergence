Charge! Phosphor 2: steer a real electron beam

The classic game borrowed its physics from a 1982 magazine listing. Here the beam follows the physics of a real cathode ray tube: electrons accelerated by kilovolts, bent by electric plates or magnetic coils, integrated step by step with the Lorentz force. Your task is the same as before, scaled up: cover the whole phosphor screen evenly and keep the coating healthy. Every control has a lesson attached.

Lessons (each has a Try it)

    
1. Volts become speed
      
An electron falling through V volts gains kinetic energy eV. Non-relativistic that gives v = sqrt(2eV/m). At tens of kilovolts the speed is a good fraction of c, so the tube uses the relativistic form. Watch speed and gamma in Live beam.

      
(gamma - 1) m c^2 = e V


    

    
2. Plates bend the path into a parabola
      
Between the plates the force is constant and sideways, so the path is a parabola. After the plates it is a straight line that appears to start at the middle of the plates (dashed line). The spot moves in proportion to plate voltage and in inverse proportion to anode voltage: raise the anode voltage and you need more plate volts for the same deflection.

      
y = (L l / 2 d) (Vp / Va)


    

    
3. The beam hits its own plates
      
Inside the plates the beam is already off axis, so a big enough voltage steers it into the metal. Real oscilloscope tubes flare the plates outward and add post-deflection acceleration. Tick Flared plates and compare.

      
    

    
4. Magnets bend without speeding up
      
The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity, so it changes direction and never speed. Compare speed before and after in Live beam: unchanged. The path is a circular arc. Magnets reach larger angles than plates and need no electrodes in the beam, which is why televisions use them.

      
theta = e B Lb / p


    

    
5. Flat screens stretch the edges
      
With a flat screen the spot lands at l times tan(theta). Equal steps in angle then give bigger steps in position near the edges, so a plain sawtooth bunches the lines at the centre and stretches them outward (pincushion-style distortion). Real sets shape the drive into an S: slide S-correction up and watch the coverage meter. 100% undoes the tan curve exactly.

      
    

    
6. Neck shadow and overscan
      
Too little drive leaves dark borders. Too much throws light onto the aquadag and glass, and a deflected beam can run into the narrow neck before it reaches the funnel (Lost to glass). Aim for the edge cells, not past them.

      
    

    
7. Spot size, brightness and burn-in
      
Light output rises with beam current and, roughly, with anode voltage to the power 1.5. The same electrons also repel each other (space charge), so more current gives a bigger, softer spot (simplified model here). A bright dot that stays still deposits all that energy in one place and wears the phosphor out: that is burn-in, and the reason screen savers exist. Persistence is how long a spot keeps glowing after the beam leaves.
